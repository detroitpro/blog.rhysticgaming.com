## A Control Masterclass at Richmond

Pro Tour Lorwyn Eclipsed went down January 30th in Richmond with a deck that shouldn't have worked—but absolutely did. A UB control list built around Doomsday Excruciator took it down, and I'm still processing the implications. This isn't your typical "stack counterspells and grind to victory" control deck. This is something weirder, smarter, and honestly more fun to watch than I expected from control in a Standard that's been dominated by creature strategies.

## The Deck: Control That Plays Creatures Like Spells

Let me be direct: **Doomsday Excruciator as a control finisher is a power move.** The deck runs three copies, and that tells you everything about its strategy. This is a control deck that's willing to tap out, willing to go low on life total, and absolutely committed to closing the game with creatures that text like they were designed specifically to punish aggressive strategies.

The mana base is clean UB with heavy Swamp bias (11 copies!), which says a lot. You're not trying to cast multiple colors per turn. You're trying to cast one powerful thing per turn, typically something that survives removal and generates value. Four Gloomlake Verge, four Restless Reef, four Watery Grave, plus the supporting cast of Multiversal Passage and Undercity Sewers gives you the flexibility to hit your colors consistently without taking damage you can't afford.

**The creatures** are the story here:

- **Doomsday Excruciator** (3x): A flying body that exiles your graveyard when it enters. Sounds bad for a control deck until you realize—in Limited, sure, you're recycling your spells. But in Standard control? Your graveyard is mostly where dead removal spells and failed card draw attempts go. Exiling it is a feature, not a bug. It's a flying 5/4 that essentially says "I'm a permanent threat you have to answer," which is exactly what control was missing in a format full of aggressive creatures.

- **Harvester of Misery** (3x): This is the real engine. Three mana, creates a token, and every creature death triggers it? In a control deck that's running Bitter Triumph, Deadly Cover-Up, and Stack of Up (plus the meta's other removal), Harvester becomes a card advantage machine. It's not about massive tokens—it's about grinding value.

- **Superior Spider-Man** (4x): Four copies. FOUR. That's not support tech; that's the core of the strategy. Double strike + must-block clause on something that survives common removal answers? In a deck built to untap and swing for 8? This closes games fast. The "must be blocked" text is crucial in a format where you might be facing down aggressive evasive creatures of your own.

## The Control Engine

Here's where I think the deck gets clever: **it's not control in the stall-until-you-win sense.** It's control in the "I answer everything you do, then I kill you with flying creatures" sense.

The instant-speed removal suite—Bitter Triumph (3x), Deadly Cover-Up (2x), the single Archenemy's Charm—gives you flexibility. You're not committed to one answer. Bitter Triumph can be proactive destruction or reactive. Deadly Cover-Up scales perfectly into the late game where you're happy to spend extra mana to exiles threats.

**Requiting Hex** (4x) is the glue here. Four mana, repeatable effect, and it scales with your graveyard strategy? In a control deck, this is your cantrip. It's your "I have a plan" button that replaces itself while dealing with threats.

Stock Up (3x) is pure cardinality—it's filling your hand while your creatures chip away. Insatiable Avarice (3x) is the same story at different mana costs. Deceit (4x) is the tempo piece: casting your opponent's creatures is absurd value in a format where good creatures exist.

## Where This Fits: A Control Player's Rebellion

Standard right now is aggressive. We've all felt it. But here's the thing about this format's aggro: it relies on synergies and synergies break to efficient removal. UB control showed up with 15 removal spells (counting everything from Bitter Triumph through Archenemy's Charm) and a creatures-as-payload approach.

The deck doesn't try to stabilize at 1 life. It stabilizes at 12, 14, 15—wherever it needs to be—and then wins through combat. That's almost elegant in its brutality.

Winternight Stories (2x) at the end of the deck list might seem weird, but in a control mirror or against other grindy matchups? Board wipes at instant speed on a stick? You're laughing. You're grinding. You're winning.

## Why This Works (And Why It Matters)

I keep coming back to one thing: **this deck proved that control doesn't have to play like it did five years ago.** You don't need to blank every threat into a win condition. You can have the better answers AND the better creatures. You can play removal all game and win in combat.

The Doomsday Excruciator pilot understood their format perfectly. They built a deck that:
1. Answers everything the format throws at it
2. Has a clear, efficient win condition (flying creatures)
3. Generates card advantage while removing threats (Harvester)
4. Scales from early game removal to late-game utility

Winning Pro Tour with this? It's a statement that control isn't dead—it's evolved.

## Personal Take

I'm genuinely excited about this. As someone getting back into competitive Magic, watching control take down a Pro Tour with a list that has *character* is refreshing. It's not the boring "have the answer, pass turn" strategy I was worried control had become. It's a carefully constructed machine that does two things simultaneously: answers threats and kills the opponent.

The creature choices especially fascinate me. Doomsday Excruciator as a finisher instead of a desperation play. Harvester of Misery as a grind engine. Superior Spider-Man as the inevitable threat that closes the game. These aren't flashy cards. They're smart cards in the right shell.

And that Gloomlake Verge mana base? Efficient, focused, exactly the color requirements the deck needs. No wasted slots. No mana base sprawl. This was tuned.

## The Takeaway

Pro Tour Lorwyn Eclipsed crowned a control deck because someone built the right control deck for THIS Standard, not for some hypothetical perfect Standard. They understood the metagame, they built for the threats that exist, and they had a repeatable, efficient way to close the game.

If you're brewing control and you're feeling frustrated, take notes from this list. Flying creatures, efficient answers, and card advantage at every level. That's the template.

Control is alive. And it's smarter than you thought.
